CHAPTER 1The puzzle

A source spits out two particles in opposite directions. Two distant scientists each measure their particle. The pattern of results is the puzzle.

§1.1Two particles fly apart

Imagine a small device sitting on a table. Every now and then, it emits two tiny particles in exactly opposite directions — one to the left, one to the right. Think of the source as if it were splitting something and shooting the halves outward. We don't need to worry about how it works. What matters is the rule: the two particles are connected, in some way, by their shared origin.

§1.2A dial and a light

Far from the source, on each side, there is a measurement box. Inside each box, the particle is fed into a piece of equipment that has two parts:

  • A dial. The scientist running the box can turn this dial to any angle they like — 0°, 17°, 90°, anything. Set it before the particle arrives.
  • A light. After the particle goes through, the light flashes one of two colours. We'll call them ▲ up and ▼ down.

That's the whole apparatus. Nothing else comes out of the box. No number, no degree, no continuous reading. Just a single binary flash: up or down. The dial is the only thing the scientist gets to choose; the flash is the only thing they observe.

Two scientists run two such boxes — Alice on one side of the lab, Bob on the other. They press the button on the source. A pair of particles flies out. Alice's particle goes left, Bob's goes right. Both boxes flash. Alice writes down what her dial was set to and which way her light flashed; Bob does the same on his side. They press the button again. And again. Thousands of times.

§1.3Comparing results

At the end of the day, Alice and Bob meet up and compare their notebooks. They are not interested in their individual flashes — Alice's flashes come out roughly half-up, half-down regardless of where she set her dial, and so do Bob's. There is no pattern visible to either of them alone.

What is interesting is the comparison between them. They line up their notebooks trial by trial and ask: when both of them set their dials to the same angle, did their lights flash the same way, or different ways?

That alone isn't the strange part — we'll see in the next chapter that there's a very simple "cheat" that could explain it. The strange part is what happens when the dials don't match. As Alice and Bob start using different angles on the two sides, the matching breaks down — but it breaks down in a precise pattern. The pattern depends only on the angle between the two dials.

Why this curve specifically

The shape of the curve isn't an experimental accident. Quantum mechanics predicts exactly this shape: at angle θ, the lights match a fraction sin²(θ/2) of the time. (You don't need the formula — just the picture: smooth, rising, with the values 0%, 50%, 100% at 0°, 90°, 180°.) The whole drama of Bell's paper is whether any "common-sense" theory could produce this same curve. Spoiler: no.

§1.4What bothered Einstein

Einstein looked at this experiment in 1935, with two collaborators (Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen), and felt something was deeply wrong with quantum mechanics. His worry went like this:

Suppose Alice sets her dial to 30° and her light flashes ▲. We already know, from the same-dial trials, that if Bob's dial is also at 30° his light must flash ▼. So the answer to "what would Bob's box have done at 30°" is determined the moment Alice's box flashes — even if Bob's box is on Mars and Alice's box is on Earth.

Einstein's question: how does Bob's particle know what to do? Two options:

  1. The particle was carrying its answer all along — like a sealed envelope it had with it from the moment it left the source. Alice's measurement just reveals which envelope she happened to get; Bob's particle has the matching one. Boring, classical, sensible. This is the "hidden variable" or "secret tag" idea.
  2. The act of Alice measuring her particle somehow reaches across space and tells Bob's particle what to flash. Faster than light. Spooky. Einstein famously called this "spooky action at a distance" and dismissed it.

Einstein, naturally, preferred option 1. He concluded that quantum mechanics must therefore be incomplete — the theory simply hadn't yet figured out what was written on the envelopes. Add the "hidden variables," and the spookiness goes away.

For thirty years, this looked like a philosophical question that physics couldn't answer. Then in 1964 John Bell did something nobody had thought possible: he found an experiment that distinguishes between option 1 and option 2. The next two chapters walk through it.
