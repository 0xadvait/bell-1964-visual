CHAPTER 2The glove analogy

The boring explanation: each particle carries its answers with it, like a sealed envelope. Let's see how far this idea gets us.

§2.1Gloves in boxes

Forget particles for a moment. Imagine instead that I have a pair of gloves — one left, one right. I put each glove in an identical-looking sealed box, shake both boxes around, and post one to Alice in London and the other to Bob in Tokyo. Neither of them knows which glove they are getting.

Alice opens her box first. She finds, say, a left glove. Instantly, without anything reaching across the world, she knows Bob's glove is the right one. If Bob's box is then opened, sure enough — right glove. The "correlation" between their results is perfect. They always get opposite gloves.

This is the boring explanation Einstein wanted for the EPR experiment. Maybe each pair of particles is like a left-right glove pair — each particle leaves the source already carrying a complete instruction sheet for what to do at any dial setting it might encounter. When Alice's box flashes ▲, that's just her opening her envelope and reading what was written inside; Bob's particle has the matching instruction sheet, so his box flashes ▼.

No spooky communication. No faster-than-light signalling. Just a shared origin and a sealed envelope. This is what physicists call a "local hidden-variable theory" — "local" because nothing crosses the gap between Alice and Bob; "hidden variables" because there are extra properties (the envelope contents) that quantum mechanics didn't bother to track.

§2.2The "secret tag" idea, in detail

Let's try to build a hidden-variable theory that actually reproduces the experiment. We need to specify, for every particle, what its "envelope" says. The envelope has to give the answer for any dial setting Alice might choose — 0°, 17°, 90°, 113°, anything — because she can choose late in the game.

Here's a simple proposal. Each particle carries one number — an angle, let's call it the particle's secret tag. When the particle reaches a measurement box, the box compares its dial setting to the secret tag and flashes ▲ or ▼ depending on whether the dial points "near" or "far" from the tag.

For Alice and Bob's same-dial trials, this model gives perfect anti-matching, which is exactly what the experiment shows. So far, so good. The "secret tag" theory passes the easy test.

§2.3When it almost works

Now let Alice and Bob choose different dial angles, and let's count up how often they match. Take many particle pairs, with the secret tag λ pointing in random directions evenly. Walk through the geometry:

  • If Alice's dial is at 0° and Bob's is at 0° (no difference): they always anti-match. Match rate = 0%.
  • If Alice's dial is at 0° and Bob's at 90°: each independently splits the circle in half, and the splits don't agree. Match rate = 50%.
  • If Alice's at 0° and Bob's at 180°: they always match. Match rate = 100%.
  • For an angle in between, the match rate depends linearly on the angle. The full curve is a straight line from 0% at 0° to 100% at 180°.

So the simple secret-tag model isn't quite right — it gets three points and misses the rest. You might object: "okay, that specific hidden-variable rule isn't right, but maybe a cleverer one is." That's exactly Bell's question. He shows that no matter how clever the rule, no local hidden-variable theory can produce the curve. There is something the curve is doing that no straight-line, sealed-envelope explanation can match.

Why this matters

It's tempting to read the gap in the picture above and think "well, maybe a different hidden-variable model would give a curvier line." Bell's contribution was to prove there is a universal upper bound on how curvy any local hidden-variable line can be — and the experimental curve sits well above that bound. No clever construction can fix it. That proof is chapter 3.
