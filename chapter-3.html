CHAPTER 3The clever experiment

Bell's idea: instead of using two dial settings, use three. There's a counting argument that any sealed-envelope theory has to obey — and the universe, as a matter of fact, doesn't.

§3.1Three dial settings

Bell's key insight is that asking "do they match?" with just two dial settings (Alice's and Bob's) doesn't give you enough information to catch a sealed-envelope theory cheating. You need three directions in the picture, and you need to count carefully.

So pick three angles, evenly spaced. Let's call them A = 0°, B = 60°, and C = 120°. On every trial, Alice randomly picks one of these three for her dial, and Bob independently does the same.

For each trial, Alice writes down which direction she picked (A, B, or C) and what her light flashed (▲ or ▼). Bob does the same. After many trials, they have two long tables.

§3.2The counting argument

Now imagine, for a moment, that the sealed-envelope explanation is correct. Each particle pair leaves the source carrying complete instructions: a triple specifying what the particle would do at each of the three possible dial settings. There are only eight such triples — every cell is either ▲ or ▼, three cells per particle.

Now translate that into Alice and Bob's notebook. Whenever Alice picks dial X and Bob picks dial Y (with X ≠ Y), they're effectively comparing two of the three columns on the same hidden sheet. Alice's flash is whatever's in column X; Bob's flash is the opposite of whatever's in column Y (because Bob's particle carries the inverted sheet). For Alice and Bob's flashes to match, the two columns of the original sheet must be different.

Looking at FIG 3.2: out of every 6-of-8 mixed sheets, two of three column-pairs are different and one is the same. Across ALL sheets (mixed + uniform), the fraction of column-pairs that are different is at most 2/3 — which means Alice and Bob's match rate, averaged across all the cross-dial trials (X≠Y), is at most 2/3 ≈ 66.7%.

Bell's bound (the simple form). In any sealed-envelope theory, when Alice and Bob use different dials randomly chosen from {A,B,C}, the fraction of trials where their flashes match must be ≤ 2/3. EQ 3.1

§3.3Where the universe breaks the rule

Now compute what quantum mechanics actually predicts for these three dial settings. Alice and Bob's dial difference, when they pick different ones, is always 60° or 120° (because the three directions are 60° apart on a clock face). So far so good — quantum mechanics actually predicts a lower match rate than Bell's bound at first glance. But hold on. The Bell bound applies to cross-dial trials only (different dial settings). When Alice and Bob both pick the same dial, they always get opposite flashes — match rate = 0%. The interesting comparison is the cross-dial-only average:

  • QM at 60° apart: 25% match
  • QM at 120° apart: 75% match
  • Average across cross-dial trials: (25 + 25 + 75 + 75 + 75 + 75) / 6 = 50%

That's still below 2/3. So this particular three-direction setup doesn't actually catch the violation — it satisfies Bell's simple counting bound. The actual violation appears with a slightly different choice of angles. The version Bell explicitly works out in his paper uses three directions where two pairs are 90° apart and one pair is 0° apart (i.e., the same direction). But the cleanest modern version is CHSH, due to Clauser, Horne, Shimony, and Holt in 1969 — it uses four directions and a slightly different counting rule.

The bottom line

It doesn't matter exactly how you set up the dials. Bell's argument has been re-derived a hundred times over the past 60 years using different angles and different counting tricks. Every version has the same shape: a hard upper bound on what a sealed-envelope theory can produce, and a quantum prediction that smashes through it. Once experimentalists actually built the setup, they found the universe sides with quantum mechanics every single time.
